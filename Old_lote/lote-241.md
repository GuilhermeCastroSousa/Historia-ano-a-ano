Habitation here goes back far beyond written history, with excavations at Jericho showing settlement and farming as early as 8000 BC. Due to its position, Canaan has been described as “the land between”, serving as a crucial point of connection between the Kingdom of Egypt to the south, the Anatolian Hittites to the north, the empires of Babylon and Assyria to the east, and the Aegean Minoans and Mycenaeans to the far west. As such, Canaan became a place where the spheres of these great powers converged and overlapped, creating a diverse meeting point of cultures, ethnicities and identities. The inhabitants of Canaan were known as Canaanites - but understanding who they were is no simple endeavour. Beneath the broad definition of an ancient Semitic-speaking civilisation lay a complex patchwork of kingdoms, city-states and communities, each with their own histories and cultural variations. As far as we can tell, the residents of Canaan did not conceive of themselves as ‘Canaanites’, or regard themselves as belonging to a single collective. Yet these diverse peoples shared many artistic, religious and social traditions, while centuries of migration and interaction with neighbouring powers shaped their culture.

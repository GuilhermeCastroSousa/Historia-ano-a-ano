descricao: What happens when Babylonian scribes start writing Akkadian and Sumerian using the Greek alphabet? The result is one of the strangest sets of tablets ever discovered — the Graeco-Babyloniaca. Were they teaching tools, experiments, or a bridge between worlds? The Graeco-Babyloniaca are some of the most fascinating artifacts from late Babylonian history. At first glance, they look like ordinary clay tablets inscribed with Akkadian or Sumerian in cuneiform. But on the reverse side, the same passages appear written phonetically in the Greek alphabet. Some tablets even contain only Greek. For years, scholars believed these were exercises by Greek students trying to master Mesopotamian languages. But in the 1970s, Assyriologist Joachim Oelsner argued they were actually written by Babylonians. Their spoken language may have been Aramaic, but they used the Greek alphabet as a tool to capture Akkadian and Sumerian sounds—especially vowel length and quality—that cuneiform and Aramaic script could not easily record. The tablets were also found in temple contexts, linked to Babylonian priestly families. Why does this matter? These tablets reveal the multilingual world of Hellenistic Mesopotamia, where scribes trained in Akkadian, Aramaic, and Greek while navigating both their ancient traditions and the new realities of Greek rule. They also give us rare insight into how Akkadian and Sumerian were pronounced long after they ceased to be everyday languages.
